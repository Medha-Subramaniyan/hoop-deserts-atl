The Court Was
Never the Constraint

Two cities built basketball courts on opposite theories of who deserves public space. Neither theory changed who plays.

01

02

The gap that is real, in both cities

Median share of adults reporting no physical activity outside work, across the same four quartiles. Two opposite distribution strategies. One identical outcome.

Physical inactivity — median % of adults
Atlanta Chicago

Near-parallel steep declines, regardless of where the courts went. If access to courts drove physical activity, these two charts could not both be true. Chicago falls 37.0% → 13.4% while its court density also falls; Atlanta falls 31.1% → 13.8% while its court density rises. The gradient is indifferent to the infrastructure.

04

"Low income" is not one population — and the courts followed only one of them

Median racial and ethnic composition of each income quartile. Chicago's Q2 is its Hispanic quartile — and court density there has already dropped from 2.11 to 1.55.

Median share of tract population
White Black Hispanic

Grouped, never stacked. ACS race (B02001) and Hispanic origin (B03003) are separate universes — a Hispanic resident is also counted in a race category, so these three shares do not sum to 100%.

Court density correlates +0.19 with % Black but −0.12 with % Hispanic in Chicago. The Park District's investment tracked Black neighborhoods, not low-income neighborhoods generally. Little Village, New City and Gage Park — Mexican-American areas with among the worst inactivity in the city — sit outside the pattern entirely.

05

A court is not a court

Among courts where the number of hoops is recorded, Atlanta is 60% full-court. Chicago is 87%. By raw count the two cities look comparable. They are not building the same thing.

Court type, where recorded
Full court (2+ hoops) Half court

Only 47 Atlanta and 98 Chicago courts have hoops recorded. Directional, not conclusive.

What nobody records
Atlanta Chicago

Lighting determines whether a court is usable after work in winter. It is recorded on 18 of 191 Atlanta courts and 14 of 387 in Chicago.

This is the thesis, not a caveat. The variable most likely to explain who actually plays is almost entirely absent from the public record of public space. Every parks bond and every "courts built" metric optimizes for what is easy to count. Presence is legible. Hours, lighting, maintenance, programming and perceived safety are not — so they do not get funded, and they do not get measured.

Method & limitations

Sources. Court locations from OpenStreetMap via the Overpass API. Health estimates from CDC PLACES 2025 — model-based small-area estimates, not direct measurement. Income, poverty and race from ACS 2023 5-year estimates. Tract geometry from Census TIGER 2025.

Scope. Chicago is restricted to city limits (781 tracts), not Cook County. The Overpass export only ever covered the city, and 97.5% of mapped courts fall inside it; including the 551 unqueried suburban tracts would have rendered them as court-free, a visual claim that is simply false. Atlanta covers Fulton and DeKalb counties (530 tracts). Income quartiles are computed within each city, so "Q1" means "the poorest quarter of this city" — which is what makes the two panels comparable.

Statistics. Correlations reported are Spearman's ρ. With 69–77% of tracts having no court, Pearson's r is distorted by outliers — in Atlanta it reported +0.157 (p<0.001) against poverty where Spearman found −0.001 (p=0.98). Rates are population-weighted, never averages of tract-level rates.

Limitations. OpenStreetMap depends on volunteer mapping and likely undercounts courts — plausibly more so in lower-income areas, which would make the reported pattern conservative, not overstated. Court attributes are sparsely recorded (see section 05). Two cities is a comparison, not a national claim. 27 tracts have suppressed income estimates and are excluded from income views.
